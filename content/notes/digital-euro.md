The debate over the digital euro is usually framed as technical or monetary. The more interesting questions, though, are political-economic: how a retail Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) is designed determines how far it pulls deposits away from commercial banks, and who ultimately bears that cost.

## The core tension

**The claim.** A retail digital euro creates a direct structural pipeline for citizens to move their money from private commercial banks to the ultimate safety of the European Central Bank (ECB). Without strict design guardrails, this will trigger systemic bank disintermediation — structurally weakening commercial banks and forcing the ECB into an accidental role as the Eurozone's primary financial allocator.

**The evidence.** When you deposit money into a traditional bank, that money becomes a liability on the bank's balance sheet, which they use to fund local loans and drive economic growth. A digital euro flips this dynamic. In times of economic anxiety, the rational consumer choice is to flee to absolute safety. Because central bank money carries zero default risk, money would inevitably flow rapidly out of commercial retail accounts and into CBDC wallets.

To prevent a permanent "digital bank run," the ECB has actively debated strict holding limits — proposing caps anywhere between €3,000 and €4,000 per citizen. Furthermore, the *reverse waterfall* mechanism ensures that any funds received above this cap automatically trickle down into a linked commercial bank account.

**Why it matters.** This tension exposes a fundamental paradox in modern monetary design. If the ECB makes the digital euro too attractive — high holding limits, yielding interest — it hollows out commercial banks, starves private credit lending, and centralizes financial power. If they make it too restrictive — low caps, no programmability, friction-heavy utility — it becomes a *ghost tool*: a solution looking for a problem that European citizens will simply ignore in favour of existing digital payment rails.

The design of the digital euro isn't just an engineering challenge. It is a profound political choice about the future architecture of European capitalism.
