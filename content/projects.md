## Ribat — Geopolitical Risk Exposure Map (2026)

An interactive map I built to show how much geopolitical risk each country actually *bears* through its trade, rather than where the risk is *emitted*. It combines the Caldara–Iacoviello GPR index with WITS/Comtrade trade weights across four channels (all trade, energy, critical materials, and shipping chokepoints), so you can see who is most exposed and toggle between channels to understand why.

[Read the story, then explore the map →](https://sam0urr.github.io/ribat/story.html)

## The Visible Hand: Funding Regimes and Retained Control in Multi-Level Integration Governance — Antwerp and Rotterdam Compared (2026)

Research paper (my MSc thesis at the University of Amsterdam). It compares how Antwerp and Rotterdam fund local integration policy and asks which level of government ends up holding control.

## Socially Just Climate Adaptation in Venserpolder — commissioned by the City of Amsterdam (2022)

Applied research and design commission from the City of Amsterdam's Urban Planning & Sustainability Department (Climate Adaptation Programme), delivered through the University of Amsterdam's *Rethinking Sustainable Societies: Urban Lab* with the Institute for Interdisciplinary Studies. The client's question: *how do we arrive at a socially just climate adaptation plan in the already built-up city?* Our case was Venserpolder, a designated *ontwikkelbuurt* (priority development neighbourhood) in Amsterdam Zuidoost where heat stress and waterlogging overlap with low incomes, high crime and weak social cohesion.

The brief sat in a gap the municipality had identified itself. The national Delta Plan on Spatial Adaptation and Amsterdam's Climate Adaptation Strategy and Implementation Agenda are strong on the ecological and economic legs of sustainability, but the social floor of the *Stadsdonut* (the city's adoption of Raworth's Doughnut Economics) was barely operationalised, and climate adaptation was not yet embedded in Circular Area Development or the *Omgevingsvisie 2050* commitment to *samen stad maken*. Our task was to make that social lower limit concrete for one neighbourhood.

Working in a five-person team over four weeks, we ran a full design-thinking cycle (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test) with the district's Green & Blue programme manager as client contact. We triangulated the city's climate stress-test maps with street-level observation, stakeholder mapping and interviews with residents, the neighbourhood broker, the homeowners' association and resident-led initiatives. On that evidence we designed a blue-green infrastructure intervention: converting the two most heat- and flood-stressed streets into canal parks with community gardens, youth facilities and a community building, grounded in the urban-heat-island and nature-based-solutions literature and checked against parking capacity and the traffic network. We then prototyped the design visually and tested it with residents.

The most useful finding came from that test. Residents rejected the scheme as *socially unjust*: not because it was wrong on climate, but because a capital-intensive landscape intervention would divert investment from safety, income and housing quality, and because earlier municipal projects had lapsed and eroded trust. Read through the distinction between distributional, procedural and recognitional justice, the binding constraint in Venserpolder is procedural: who is in the room when adaptation money is allocated. Our recommendations to the client therefore paired the long-term blue-green plan with a short-term route that builds on existing resident-led greening, expands community-centre capacity, and gives active residents a clear division of responsibilities with the municipality. The report and presentation were delivered to the City of Amsterdam.

*Frameworks: Delta Plan on Spatial Adaptation · Stadsdonut / Doughnut Economics · Circular Area Development · Omgevingsvisie 2050 · design thinking and co-creation · blue-green infrastructure · procedural justice*
